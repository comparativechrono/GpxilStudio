Draw a gene, a region or a chromosome

A gpxil file is already a picture: each base is a pixel. Drawing a region means choosing which bases, how to colour them and how to lay them out. This walkthrough draws HG002’s TP53 in each of the four views, a satellite repeat that appears only when the rows are the right width, and a whole chromosome, the mitochondrion’s.

1 · Find the region in the Viewer

The Viewer is the quickest way to find a region worth drawing. Type a region such as chr17:7,661,779-7,687,546 (TP53), move along with the arrows, and zoom with + and −. Under the picture, the Viewer gives the gpxil render command that draws what you see as a file, and Save the picture saves the view as it is.

Regions count from 1 and include both ends, as in a genome browser: chr17:7,661,779-7,687,546 is 25,768 bases. A contig’s name alone means all of it.

2 · Draw it

Draw a region as a picture writes the region as a PNG (or a TIFF). For a region the size of a gene, it suggests 256 bases a row and 4 pixels a base, so each base is a square you can see.

3 · The four views

ShowWhat each pixel is
referencethe reference’s base, in the palette’s colours. Bases in soft-masked repeats (lower case in the reference) are paler, an unknown base (N) is near-black, in the classic colours, and a gap is paper.
genotypethe person’s two bases at once: where both copies agree, their base’s colour; where they differ, a mix of the two colours. A base deleted from both copies is paper.
haplotypesthe two copies separately, in two rows of pixels for each row of bases: the first haplotype above the second. With Mark where insertions are, a base that an insertion follows is magenta.
variantsthe reference, faded, with the person’s variants on top: heterozygous orange, homozygous red, a deletion dark, a base an insertion follows green, no call grey, a complex variant purple.

The Colours are one of gpxil’s palettes: classic (A red, C blue, G green, T yellow), okabe-ito, whose colours people with colour-blindness can tell apart, gpxi, the greys of Albrecht-Buehler’s original genome pixel images, and process, the four printing inks. The palettes gives their exact colours.

4 · The width of the rows

The width of the rows changes nothing in the data, only how it is laid out, but it can change what you see. A repeat whose unit is as long as a row lines up in columns. Near the middle of chromosome 17 lies alpha satellite, the repeat that makes up centromeres, in units of 171 bases. At 171 bases a row it shows as vertical stripes; at 200 it looks like noise.

In the form, set Bases per row to 171, then to 200, and compare the two pictures. This is how Albrecht-Buehler found repeats by eye: by wrapping a sequence at the width of its period.

5 · A Hilbert curve

In rows, bases that are next to each other in the genome can end up far apart in the picture: the last base of one row and the first of the next. A Hilbert curve layout folds the sequence into a square so that neighbours stay neighbours, and any stretch of the sequence is a compact blob. A gene shows as a patch rather than a band.

(Choose a Hilbert curve under Layout.) TP53’s 25,768 bases fill a square of 256 by 256 pixels, with room to spare.

6 · A whole chromosome

The mitochondrial chromosome is small enough to draw whole: 16,569 bases, its name alone as the region.

In the browser, a picture may have up to 25 million bases and 64 million pixels: a region of any chromosome, but not a large chromosome in one go (chromosome 21, the shortest autosome, has 46.7 million bases). To see a whole chromosome at one dot a base, print it as a book (Print a chromosome as a book); on your own computer, gpxil render has no such limit.

7 · For print

A PNG is for the screen. For print, choose TIFF in CMYK under Picture: each base’s colour is then given directly in ink, in the palette’s own CMYK values, rather than converted from screen colours. Give the Dots per inch you will print at, and with one pixel a base, each base is one printed dot.

The same in Python

render returns the picture as palette indices, with its screen and print colour tables, so you can scale it, save it, or look at it in a notebook:

from gpxiltools import GpxilFile
from gpxiltools.render import render

with GpxilFile("/data/examples/HG002_chr17.gpxil.tif") as g:
    picture = render(g["chr17"], mode="variants", start=7_661_778, end=7_687_546, bases_per_row=256)
picture = picture.scaled(2)
picture.save_png("/home/user/results/TP53_from_python.png")
print(picture.shape, picture.title)
for label, index in picture.legend:
    print(label, picture.rgb[index])
